What makes it different

Most calculators leave these out.

Lean mass BMR

Uses the Katch-McArdle formula — based on lean mass, not total bodyweight. Two people at the same weight with different body fat percentages have different metabolisms.

Thermic effect of food

Protein burns 25% of its calories just to digest. Carbs 7.5%, fat 1.5%. This is solved iteratively alongside your macros. Most TDEE calculators skip it entirely.

Goal body fat %, not weight

Set a target body fat percentage instead of a scale number. The calculator accounts for lean mass loss during the cut and reverse-engineers the correct goal weight.

Experience-scaled surplus

Beginners bulk at +400 cal, intermediates +300, advanced +200. Matched to realistic muscle gain rates so extra calories don't just become fat.

Deficit-adjusted LBM model

On a cut, expected muscle loss scales with both your experience level and how aggressive your deficit is — not a fixed assumption across all users.

Training + nutrition unified

Volume, intensity, progression strategy, recommended split, and macros all in one place. No switching between separate calculators.

Research & Methodology

The science behind every output.

Every formula and recommendation is grounded in peer-reviewed research. This page walks through the methodology — from nutrition targets to training volume to the exact calculations behind each output.

Nutrition

Macronutrient targets

Protein — Cut: 1.2g/lb LM  |  Bulk: 1.0g/lb LM

Elevated protein during restriction

During a caloric deficit, protein needs rise to prevent lean mass loss. Research shows intakes of 1.8–2.7 g/kg during restriction preserve significantly more muscle. Protein is set from lean mass — not total bodyweight — because only muscle tissue requires it.

↗ Helms et al. (2014) — Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab ↗ Longland et al. (2016) — Am J Clin Nutr
Fat — 0.4–0.5g/lb bodyweight

Dietary fat & hormone support

Low-fat diets significantly reduce testosterone. A meta-analysis of 6 intervention studies found meaningful decreases in androgens on low-fat vs high-fat diets. Cholesterol — supplied by dietary fat — is the direct precursor for steroid hormone synthesis.

↗ Whittaker & Wu (2021) — J Steroid Biochem Mol Biol
TEF — 25% / 7.5% / 1.5%

Thermic effect of food

Digesting food burns calories — protein at 20–30% of its caloric content, carbs 5–10%, fat 0–3%. Most TDEE calculators skip this. The TEF is solved iteratively alongside macros since each depends on the other.

↗ Westerterp (2004) — Nutrition & Metabolism
Surplus — 200–400 cal by experience

Experience-scaled calorie surplus

A surplus maximizes muscle gain, but larger surpluses increase fat without proportionally more muscle. The 200/300/400 values are derived from these principles combined with documented diminishing returns in muscle gain with training age.

↗ Ribeiro et al. (2019) — PMC ↗ Barakat et al. (2023) — PMC
Bulk partitioning — 70% lean / 30% fat

Lean mass gain ratio

During a caloric surplus with resistance training, weight gained splits between lean mass and fat. A pilot study found surplus produced more lean mass than maintenance, but also more fat — lean mass comprising roughly 30% of total mass gained.

↗ Ribeiro et al. (2019) — PMC
Deficit — 300 / 500 / 750 cal

Deficit size & LBM retention

A meta-analysis found a ~500 kcal/day deficit fully blunts lean mass gains during resistance training. Larger deficits proportionally increase lean mass losses — the basis for scaling expected muscle loss by deficit size and experience level.

↗ Murphy et al. (2021) — PubMed

Training

Training recommendations

Frequency — 2–3x per muscle

Training frequency & hypertrophy

A landmark meta-analysis established that training each muscle group at least twice per week produces superior hypertrophic outcomes compared to once per week.

↗ Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2016) — Sports Medicine
Volume — 6–22 sets/muscle/week

Volume dose-response

Hypertrophy follows a dose-response relationship with volume up to a recoverable ceiling. Israetel's volume landmark framework (MEV, MAV, MRV) and Schoenfeld's meta-analysis form the basis for the experience-differentiated ranges.

↗ Schoenfeld et al. (2017) — J Sports Sci
Intensity — RPE 7–9.5

Proximity to failure

RPE provides a practical intensity metric. Beginners train further from failure to build motor patterns; advanced lifters train closer to maintain the hypertrophic stimulus as the adaptive ceiling rises.

↗ Schoenfeld & Grgic (2019) — Strength & Cond J
Recomposition

Simultaneous muscle gain & fat loss

A systematic review confirmed recomposition in resistance-trained individuals across multiple RCTs. Rate is highest in beginners and declines sharply with experience.

↗ Barakat et al. (2020) — Strength & Conditioning Journal

Formulas

How it's calculated.

A plain-English walkthrough of every calculation that runs when you hit Generate. Each step feeds into the next.

Step 1

How many calories does your body burn at rest?

Most calculators use total bodyweight — but that ignores body composition. The Katch-McArdle formula uses lean mass only, making it more accurate for people at different body fat levels.

Lean Mass (kg) = bodyweight × (1 − BF%) × 0.454
Resting Calories = 370 + (21.6 × Lean Mass in kg)
Example: 180 lbs at 15% BF → 153 lbs lean mass → ~68 kg → resting burn of ~1,838 cal/day
Step 2

How many calories do you burn moving around?

Your resting burn is the baseline. On top of that, your body burns calories through everyday movement (NEAT) and actual workouts, scaled separately.

Daily movement = Resting Calories × activity multiplier
// Sedentary × 1.2  |  Moderately Active × 1.35  |  Very Active × 1.5
Workout calories = (training days/week × 300) ÷ 7
Step 3

How many calories does digestion burn?

Digesting food costs calories. Protein is the most expensive at 25%, carbs 7.5%, fat 1.5%. Because TEF depends on macros which depend on total calories which include TEF — this is solved iteratively until the result stabilizes.

TEF = (Protein_kcal × 0.25) + (Carb_kcal × 0.075) + (Fat_kcal × 0.015)
Maintenance = NEAT + Exercise + TEF
// Solved iteratively until convergence
Step 4

What's your daily calorie target?

Once maintenance is known, the target is set based on your goal. Bulk surplus scales smaller with experience — advanced lifters' adaptive ceiling is lower, so excess calories beyond ~200/day mostly become fat.

Cut: Maintenance − 300 / 500 / 750 cal
Bulk: Maintenance + 400 / 300 / 200 cal (Beginner / Inter / Adv)
Recomp: Maintenance
Step 5

How are calories split into macros?

Protein and fat are set first as minimums based on lean mass and bodyweight. Every remaining calorie goes to carbohydrates to fuel workouts and maintain anabolic hormone levels.

Cut / Recomp:  Protein = LM × 1.2g  |  Fat = BW × 0.4g
Bulk:          Protein = LM × 1.0g  |  Fat = BW × 0.5g
Carbs = (Target Cals − P×4 − F×9) ÷ 4
Step 6

Where does the weight change come from?

Not all weight lost on a cut is fat — some is muscle. Not all weight gained on a bulk is muscle — some is fat. The model accounts for both based on experience level and deficit or surplus size.

Cut: LBM_lost = totalWeightLost × ratio
// Beginner 0–10%  |  Intermediate 10–20%  |  Advanced 15–30%

Bulk: LBM_gained = totalGain × 0.70  |  Fat_gained = totalGain × 0.30
Step 7

How long will it take?

One pound of body fat contains roughly 3,500 calories. Dividing the total calorie gap by your daily deficit gives the number of weeks to goal.

Weeks = (pounds to lose × 3,500) ÷ (daily deficit × 7)
Example: 20 lbs at −500 cal/day → (20 × 3,500) ÷ (500 × 7) = 20 weeks
